<strong>Parent WhatsApp groups: what the school can do and which channel to offer</strong>

<br>

Every September the same scene repeats: on the first day of class someone creates the class WhatsApp group and, within a week, it already has forty participants, two hundred messages, and at least one argument. On 8 September, the Spanish Consumers' Association devoted its back-to-school campaign to asking families to use these groups responsibly: not to criticise the school, the teachers, or other families, and not to turn them into the school diary the student should be carrying.

For a school, the useful question is which part of what happens in those groups falls to it, and what it can offer so they stop being the channel where everyone finds things out.

<br>

<strong>What the school does not control</strong>

<br>

Family WhatsApp groups are private. The school does not create them, does not administer them, and is not responsible for what is posted in them. It also cannot ban them or force anyone to join: participation is voluntary, and the right approach is for whoever creates the group to share an invite link instead of adding people without asking.

Accepting this saves a lot of wasted effort. What is in the school's hands is something else: that those groups are not necessary in order to hear about what matters.

<br>

<strong>What the AEPD says</strong>

<br>

The Spanish Data Protection Agency's guide for schools addresses the use of instant messaging in the school setting. In short:

- **Communication between teachers and families** should preferably use the means the school provides, such as the educational platform or the school email.
- **Groups with students** are not the right tool; contact should have an educational purpose and use the school's applications.
- **Student images** should not circulate by instant messaging. The guide allows exceptions when the child's interest requires it, for example to tell a family about an accident on a trip.

The underlying reason is simple: in a WhatsApp group, the teacher's personal number, student data, and photos sit outside any control by the school, with no record of who has seen what and no way to withdraw a message sent by mistake.

<br>

<strong>Why families end up on WhatsApp</strong>

<br>

If families get their information from the group, it is almost always because the official channel fails at something:

- **It arrives late:** the paper circular shows up in the backpack three days later.
- **It is scattered:** some things arrive by email, others on the website, others in the diary.
- **It does not notify:** you have to go in and look, and nobody goes in and looks.
- **It does not reply:** a simple question takes days to resolve.

When that happens, the group fills the gap: someone photographs the circular, someone else asks what to bring tomorrow, and the official information starts circulating in different versions.

<br>

<strong>How to offer an official channel that works</strong>

<br>

The official channel wins when it is faster and more reliable than the group. That requires a few concrete things:

- **One place** for notices, circulars, authorisations, the calendar, and messages with the tutor.
- **Mobile notifications**, so the family does not have to remember to log in.
- **Messages by class or group**, so each family receives only what affects them.
- **Read confirmation** on important notices, so you know who has not seen them and can call them.
- **Sending and reply hours** known to everyone. We develop this in <a href="/en/blog/right-to-disconnect-school-family-communication-hours-2026/">the right to disconnect and communication windows</a>.
- **Translation** for families who do not read the school's language well.

With that, the WhatsApp group stops being essential. It will still exist for organising a birthday or a gift for the tutor, which is exactly what it is for.

<br>

<strong>Rules the school can suggest</strong>

<br>

Even if it does not run the groups, the school can offer some guidelines through class representatives or the parents' association. They work better if they are few and concrete:

1. The group is not an official channel of the school. Questions for the tutor go through the platform.
2. Photos or videos of other children are not shared without their families' permission.
3. Problems with a teacher or another student are dealt with through the tutor or leadership, not in the group.
4. A reasonable messaging schedule is respected.
5. Nobody is added without their consent; an invite link is shared.

Presenting them at the start-of-year meeting, as part of "how we will communicate this year", usually works better than sending them as a rule. There is a script for that meeting in <a href="/en/blog/back-to-school-family-meeting-script/">the start-of-year meeting with families</a>.

<br>

<strong>When the conflict is already in the group</strong>

<br>

Sooner or later, a message from the group reaches leadership: a complaint about a teacher, a student being singled out, or a photo that should not be there. A few guidelines:

- **Do not reply in the group.** The school is not in it and should not join the argument.
- **Move the conversation to the official channel** or to a meeting with the families involved.
- **If there are images of students** taken at the school, ask for them to be removed and remind people of the rule.
- **If there is a possible case of bullying** or a risk to a minor, activate the relevant protocol with the wellbeing and protection coordinator.

<br>

<strong>Conclusion</strong>

<br>

Family WhatsApp groups are not going to disappear, and they do not need to. What the school can achieve is that they stop being the source of official information: with its own channel that notifies, arrives on time, and replies, the group goes back to what it should be, a space between families.
