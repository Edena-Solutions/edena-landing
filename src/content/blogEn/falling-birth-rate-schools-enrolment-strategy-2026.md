<strong>Fewer children, same places: defending enrolment as birth rates fall</strong>

<br>

The numbers are uncomfortable to look at. The collapse in Spain's child population equals a theoretical loss of more than 32,000 classrooms between 2016 and 2026. For a leadership team this is not background context: it is the conversation held every spring with the governing body, and it is usually framed wrongly, as though it were a marketing problem.

<br>

<strong>The numbers, without the drama</strong>

<br>

Worth having the figures in front of you before debating strategy, because the impact is not uniform:

- **Andalusia:** 271,785 fewer children aged three to sixteen.
- **Catalonia:** 215,466 fewer children in the same bracket.
- **Valencia:** 166,301 fewer children.
- **Madrid:** 120,524 fewer children.
- **Rural areas:** over a thousand municipalities with no children at all aged zero to four.

<br>

<strong>Why this is not a marketing problem</strong>

<br>

Demographics distribute, but they do not decide who keeps each student. In an area where 20% fewer children are born, the school that runs its process well can hold its roll while the one down the road loses two forms. Market share redistributes, and **it redistributes towards whoever has the process in order**, not towards whoever shouts loudest.

What follows is a three-front plan, ordered by return: first what you already have, then what already comes to you, and only at the end what you have to go out and find.

<br>

<strong>Front one: don't lose the families you have</strong>

<br>

Retention costs five to seven times less than recruitment, and it is the front almost every school neglects because it assumes renewal is automatic. It isn't: it is automatic until it stops being so, and by the time it stops it is late.

The metric to track is the renewal rate by year group, not the school-wide figure. A school with 95% average renewal can be running at 78% at the transition from primary to secondary, which is where families traditionally move on. Without breaking it down by year, that hole stays hidden behind a reassuring average.

<br>

<strong>The four signals that warn of a departure</strong>

<br>

A family that leaves rarely decides in June: they decide months earlier, accumulating small frictions. Four indicators can be watched without asking anyone anything:

- **A rise in unexcused absences:** disengagement almost always starts with attendance.
- **Late or returned payments:** these do not always mean financial hardship; often they mean disaffection.
- **A drop in participation:** in meetings, activities and communications with read receipts.
- **Unanswered enquiries:** the ones that came into the office and nobody closed.

Any of the four deserves a call from the tutor before spring arrives. Two of them at once is an alert.

<br>

<strong>Front two: convert what already reaches you</strong>

<br>

This is the front with the most headroom and the worst measurement. Most schools know how many enrolments they made, but not how many enquiries they received, how many visits they ran, or where the ones that never enrolled dropped out.

<br>

<strong>Where the funnel leaks</strong>

<br>

The funnel has four steps and each one leaks:

- **From enquiry to visit:** you lose the families nobody answered in time. If first contact takes more than twenty-four hours, many have already visited elsewhere.
- **From visit to application:** you lose those who got no follow-up at all afterwards.
- **From application to enrolment:** you lose those who ran into awkward or slow paperwork.
- **From enrolment to the first day:** you lose those who got a place at their first choice and never heard from you again.

Instrumenting this does not need a big investment, it needs recording: every enquiry with its date, source and owner; every visit with its outcome; every application with its status. The usual finding is that **the most expensive step is not the last one but the first**, the response time to the initial enquiry.

<br>

<strong>What not to do: cut the fees</strong>

<br>

When enrolment softens, the temptation is to touch the fee. It rarely pays off. Cutting the price fixes one campaign and compromises several years, because raising it again with enrolled families is far harder than winning them at the right price in the first place. Exhaust conversion and retention first — they cost nothing.

<br>

<strong>Front three: go out and find them, but with judgement</strong>

<br>

Only after plugging the two holes above does recruitment spending make sense, because otherwise you are paying to pour water into a leaking bucket.

<br>

<strong>The channels, ordered by cost</strong>

<br>

Once you get here, the rule is simple: invest where cost per enrolled student is measurable.

- **Recommendations from current families:** the cheapest channel and the one that converts best. It is activated by asking, not by waiting.
- **Open days:** they work if there is follow-up afterwards, and they don't if there isn't.
- **Search visibility:** it matters because a family moving to a new city always starts there.
- **Paid advertising:** the last option, not the first, because it is the only one that stops producing the day you stop paying.

<br>

<strong>The three numbers leadership should review every month</strong>

<br>

All of the above yields three indicators that fit on one screen and belong in the monthly leadership meeting:

- **Application-to-enrolment conversion**, broken down by entry year group.
- **Projected renewal** for next year, calculated from operational signals rather than stated intentions.
- **Families at risk**, where risk means two or more of the four signals stacking up.

None requires surveys or consultants: all three come from data the school already generates. The usual problem is not missing data but that it sits in four separate systems and nobody cross-references it.

<br>

<strong>Case study (Spain)</strong>

<br>

A state-funded school in a provincial capital lost a form group at the transition to secondary two years running. The governing body's initial reading was demographic: fewer children are born, nothing to be done.

Breaking down the data showed something else. Renewal in early years and primary stood at 96%, but at the jump to secondary it was 74%. And of the families who left, two thirds had shown at least two operational signals in the previous year: returned payments or a steep drop in meeting attendance.

The school changed neither its offer nor its fees. It changed when it talks to families: it moved the continuity conversation with year-six families forward to January, prioritised those with stacked signals, and gave the first call to the tutor rather than the office. The following year, renewal at the secondary transition rose to 88%. The demographics had not changed.

<br>

<strong>Conclusion</strong>

<br>

Falling birth rates are real and will last for years, but they are a fact about the environment, not a verdict on your school. What decides whether you lose a form group is not how many children are born in the province, but how precisely you know your own funnel: how many families enquire, how many you convert, how many renew, and which ones are signalling that they are leaving while there is still time to talk to them.
